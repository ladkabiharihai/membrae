# Pragnosia: a compute-efficient, brain-like, self-growing recurrent model with intrinsic faculties

*Ashish Kumar*

## Abstract

Pragnosia is not another language model to be out-scaled. It is a study of a single question: can a small, compute-efficient recurrent model be brain-like, with its faculties intrinsic to the forward pass rather than supplied by external code, and can it grow developmentally, starting small and cheap and growing into capacity rather than being trained large from scratch, all of it provable at small scale? We answer with five contributions, each backed by a committed measurement. (1) An efficient recall core, VChunkRecall, a vectorized chunk-parallel linear-attention core with a second-order Taylor feature map, reaches 100% associative recall on 16-pair MQAR (Result 30, Result 34) while running about 17 times faster than flash-attention at a 64K context (Result 34) and about 13 times faster than a parameter-matched transformer at 32K (Result 39). (2) Four faculties live in the model itself: forget-free online learning, where new facts enter the fast-weight state and a prior skill is held at 100% while gradient-based weight teaching collapses that skill to 0% (Result 31); constant-memory streaming, flat at 73 MB from 500 to 100K tokens (Result 32); agency, that is learning from consequence, at 85% exploit-after-discovery against a 25% chance floor (Result 36); and a per-FLOP advantage at long context (Result 34, Result 39). A single 2.4M-parameter model exhibits all four at once (Result 37, Result 68). (3) Function-preserving self-growth is measurably cheaper to train: growing small-then-big reaches a target loss for 17% less compute with an identity operator (Result 61), 22% with a stacking operator (Result 62), and 34% to 36% with an early-heavy schedule combined with a hybrid operator that is both function-preserving and warm-started (Result 65, Result 66); the saving holds at 31% under a threefold-larger budget and width, with recall intact at 99% (Result 69), and growth can be triggered by the model's own predictive-entropy saturation (Result 47). (4) Constant-compute sparse growth, via top-1 expert routing, holds active parameters per token flat at 8.3M as total parameters more than double, with recall faculties intact at 100% (Result 74). (5) We report scale-gated boundaries plainly: growth buys efficiency, not free capability (Result 48, Result 56); scaling is not demonstrable within our compute budget across five separate studies (Result 59); and at 216M the language product is marginal, at 25% on realistic multi-fact document question answering (Result 71), with long-context recall recovered by cheap supervised fine-tuning rather than by scale (a needle at 1792 tokens moving from 0% to 24%, Result 60). The predecessor spin-dominant recurrence is retired as a documented negative: it cannot do associative recall (26% on 4-pair MQAR, Result 30) and only ties a matched transformer at 284M parameters (Result 14).

## 1. Introduction

Most recent progress in sequence modeling has come from scale: more parameters, more tokens, more compute. This work asks a different question. Can a small model be brain-like, meaning that the faculties one associates with a cognitive system (durable memory, learning without catastrophic forgetting, adaptation from consequence, and continuous streaming) are intrinsic to its forward pass rather than provided by an external controller wrapped around it? And can such a model grow developmentally, learning cheaply while small and then adding capacity, rather than being instantiated at full size and trained from scratch? Small scale is not a limitation we tolerate here, it is the point. The deliverable is a set of principles with evidence, not a benchmark score. A model that is useless for coding or closed-book trivia at 216M parameters is irrelevant to that judgment, so long as the mechanisms it demonstrates are real and measured.

We take "intrinsic" to have a concrete, falsifiable meaning. A faculty is intrinsic if it is a property of the architecture and the forward computation, not of scaffolding code that stores facts in a Python dictionary, calls a teach() routine, or routes queries with a hand-built controller. This project began as a spin-dominant language model whose faculties were exactly that kind of external scaffolding. Honest evaluation retired both choices. The diagonal-complex spin recurrence, while fast, cannot perform associative recall (26% on 4-pair MQAR against roughly 100% for attention, Result 30), so it cannot by construction support memory or online learning, and at matched parameters it is a statistical tie with a transformer rather than a win (Result 14). We keep that outcome as a documented negative and rebuild on a substrate that can carry a brain.

That substrate is VChunkRecall (Section 3), a linear-attention core with a second-order Taylor feature map that gives it softmax-like sharpness and therefore associative recall. Its memory is literally its recurrent fast-weight state, the running sum of outer products of key and value features, and reading that state is recall while writing it is learning, so both occur inside the forward pass with no external store. The core reaches 100% on 16-pair MQAR (Result 30, Result 34) and, because it is linear in sequence length, its throughput advantage over attention grows with context: about 13 times a matched transformer at 32K tokens (Result 39), about 17 times flash-attention at 64K in training (Result 34), and about 21 times at 131K tokens in inference where attention throughput collapses quadratically (Result 70). The vectorized formulation achieves this in pure PyTorch, with no custom kernel.

On this core we build and measure four faculties, each in the model rather than around it (Section 4). Forget-free online learning follows from writing new knowledge into the fast-weight state: a model taught new facts in-state keeps a previously learned skill at 100%, whereas teaching the same facts by gradient descent into the weights drives that skill to 0% (Result 31). Constant-memory streaming follows from the recurrent state having fixed size: peak memory stays flat at 73 MB from 500 to 100K tokens (Result 32), with a recall horizon that tracks the trained sequence length rather than extending without bound (Result 36). Agency, learning from consequence, is demonstrated as in-context reinforcement, where the state adapts to reward with zero weight updates and reaches 85% exploitation after a good action has been discovered, against a 25% chance floor (Result 36). The per-FLOP efficiency win is the same linear-time property measured against attention at long context (Result 34, Result 39). A single 2.4M-parameter unified model performs all four simultaneously, and preserves them across a growth event (Result 37, Result 68).

Growth is the developmental axis, and here an early, honest correction shapes the entire claim (Section 5). We first hoped that growing a network would yield capability for free, and we recorded four negatives when it did not: adding depth to a live language run left held-out recall flat across a 216M-to-355M climb (Result 48), and a manual scale to 400M plateaued no better than the 216M it grew from (Result 56). A bigger network still needs training, so those negatives are correct. The right brain-like claim is not that growth adds capability but that growth adds efficiency, and the right metric is loss against cumulative compute (parameters times tokens), not loss against steps. Under that metric, growing small-then-big reaches a target loss for less total compute than training from scratch, because early learning happens cheaply at small size before capacity is added. We measure 17% with a function-preserving identity operator (Result 61), 22% with a stacking operator that copies trained layers (Result 62), and 34% to 36% when the schedule is front-loaded (early-heavy) and paired with a hybrid operator that is function-preserving at the moment of growth yet warm-started (Result 65, Result 66). The ordering is robust and the saving is not a small-budget artifact: it holds at 31% under a threefold-larger compute budget and larger width, with recall intact at 99% through the growth (Result 69). Growth need not follow an external schedule at all: a live run grew itself when its own predictive entropy saturated (Result 47), the label-free signal a developmental system would use. This places the work in the lineage of function-preserving growth (Net2Net) and compute-saving warm-started pretraining (bert2BERT, LiGO), inside their reported 20% to 50% band, with the distinction that the growth here runs on an efficient recurrent brain core with intrinsic faculties and can trigger itself.

We also examine a second capacity axis that adds parameters without adding per-token compute (Section 6). Sparse top-1 expert routing keeps active parameters per token flat at 8.3M while total parameters more than double, and the recall faculty survives it intact (100% on 16-pair MQAR at both four and eight experts), though the capability benefit of the extra parameters is scale-gated and does not appear at this size and training budget (Result 74).

Throughout, we report where the story stops. The faculties and the growth efficiency are strong, reproducible, small-scale wins, but scaling to a useful language model is not something our compute budget can demonstrate: five separate scaling studies, across depth, feature state, manual jumps, and width, are negative or inconclusive (Result 59). Long-context recall on real prose at 216M is limited by language grounding rather than by the recall mechanism, and it is recovered cheaply by answer-masked supervised fine-tuning (a needle at 1792 tokens moving from 0% to 24%, Result 60) rather than by growth. As a product the 216M model is marginal, at 25% on realistic multi-fact document question answering (Result 71). Adaptive compute through chain-of-thought with intrinsic halting lifts multi-step composition sharply (13% to 92%, Result 41) but belongs in a reasoning fine-tune, since it does not pretrain stably (Result 42). We state these boundaries as plainly as the positive results, because the contribution of this paper is a method and its honest evidence, and a negative that is correctly attributed is as much a part of that method as a win.

The rest of the paper is organized as follows. Section 3 describes the recall core and its efficiency. Section 4 presents the four intrinsic faculties and their measurements. Section 5 develops function-preserving self-growth and the compute-efficiency result. Section 6 covers constant-compute sparse growth. Section 7 gathers the scale-gated limits, including the retired spin substrate, as documented negatives.

## The substrate (VChunkRecall core)

The brain is built on a single recurrent core, `VChunkRecall`: a chunk-parallel linear-attention layer with a 2nd-order Taylor feature map. The feature map is the load-bearing design choice. A plain diagonal-complex ("spin") recurrence is fast but cannot retrieve a specific earlier token: on multi-query associative recall (MQAR) it scores 26%, 12%, and 2% at 4, 16, and 32 key-value pairs (Result 30). The Taylor map approximates the exponential of a query-key dot product, giving softmax-like sharpness, and with it the same-sized core reaches 100% on MQAR at 16 pairs (Result 30). This is the property that lets the core hold and read back memories at all.

The memory is the state, and writing the state is learning. Each layer accumulates a fast-weight state as the outer-product sum of feature-mapped keys and values, and recall is the feature-mapped query read against that state. Both the write and the read happen inside the forward pass, so memory and online learning are architectural rather than provided by external code (Result 30).

The core is computed in a vectorized, chunk-parallel form: all chunks' intra-chunk attention as one batched matmul and the inter-chunk state recurrence as one cumulative sum, replacing a Python loop of many small operations. It is pure PyTorch with no custom kernel. At a 65,536-token context, forward plus backward, the vectorized core runs at 1.95M tokens/s, roughly 25 times the eager chunk loop and about 17 times flash-attention (116K tokens/s), while recall at 16 pairs holds at 99% (Result 34). The earlier non-vectorized ChunkedRecall form already ran at 1.47M tokens/s, about 7 times the spin carrier (Result 30). Because the core is linear-time, its throughput advantage grows with length: measured against attention at matched width it reaches about 13 times at a 32,768-token context (Result 39) and about 21 times at 131,072 tokens (Result 70).

Recall capacity is set by the feature (Taylor) dimension, not by depth. Widening the feature dimension lifts MQAR at 16 pairs from 9.3% to 100% (feat 1 to 32), while quadrupling depth at a bottlenecked feature dimension buys essentially nothing (9.1% to 9.9%) (Result 49). The recall faculty is preserved as the core is grown wider, holding at 99% on MQAR-16 at width d=512 (Result 69).

## Intrinsic faculties

Four brain faculties were built and measured on this core at small scale. Each is a property of the forward pass, not of external scaffolding.

**Associative recall (memory).** The core retrieves planted key-value bindings from its state: 100% on MQAR at 16 pairs for ChunkedRecall (Result 30) and 99% for the vectorized VChunkRecall core (Result 34). This is the substrate-level memory the other faculties build on.

**Forget-free online learning.** A small model was trained to hold a fixed skill in its weights (a permutation lookup, 100%) and to recall facts from its state. New facts were then taught two ways. Streaming the facts through the forward pass (into the fast-weight state, weights untouched) recalled the new facts at 88% and kept the prior skill at 100%. Gradient-fitting the same facts into the weights also learned them at 88% but destroyed the prior skill, dropping it from 100% to 0% (Result 31). Because new knowledge enters the state through a forward pass, weights and all prior skills are untouched by construction. One honest recipe caveat: the skill must be trained to be state-robust (computed correctly regardless of state contents), or it breaks under a loaded state (Result 31).

**Agency (learning from consequence).** In an in-context bandit, the model explores and then exploits the reward history carried in its fast-weight state, with zero weight updates. Measured as exploitation-after-discovery (does the model repeat a good action on later visits to a context once it has been rewarded), it reaches 85% against a 25% chance baseline (Result 36). An earlier weaker figure was a metric artifact: the crude first-versus-last-third rate conflates unavoidable first-visit exploration with failure (Result 36).

**O(1) streaming.** The recurrent state gives constant memory: peak memory stays flat at 73 MB from 500 to 100,000 tokens, which a growing attention cache cannot do (Result 32). Recall over a stream is real but bounded by the trained length, not unbounded. On the fast core, training on 6K-token streams gives 100% recall at both 2K and 8K, but this falls to 2% at 32K and 0% at 100K (Result 36); the earlier core showed the same shape (100% at 8K, 67% at 32K, 2% at 100K) (Result 32). Constant memory is proven; recall extends with the trained horizon but does not extrapolate past it.

**One unified model, and survival through growth.** All four faculties were then put in a single 2.4M-parameter model trained on a mixed stream, so they are shown to coexist rather than living on separate toy networks. Before growth (3 layers) the model scores skill 100%, recall 100%, streaming 100%, agency 72%, and forget-free (new facts 50%, prior skill kept 100%). Growth is a function-preserving model method that adds a depth block as an exact identity; immediately after growing to 4 layers, before any further training, the faculties are essentially unchanged (skill 100%, recall 97%, streaming 100%, agency 75%, forget-free new 33%, kept 100%). After continued training at 4 layers, every faculty holds or improves (skill 100%, recall 98%, streaming 100%, agency 99%, forget-free new 100%, kept 100%) (Result 68). The growth-efficiency check confirms the faculty preservation at larger budget: MQAR-16 stays at 99% at width d=512 through growth (Result 69).

**Honest scope.** These are mechanism proofs at small scale on synthetic tokens. Streaming recall does not extrapolate beyond its trained horizon (Results 32, 36), and the faculties are validated as principles in one small unified model (Result 68), not at a large trained size. The substrate and the four faculties are the validated claim; language-scale transfer is reported separately and is not asserted here.

## EFFICIENCY: inference and training

The core's efficiency claim is precise: the win is compute and time at long context, driven by the linear-time (O(T)) recall core against attention's quadratic (O(T^2)) forward. It is not a memory win. We state each axis with its measured limit.

### Inference throughput

Measured at matched size (d=512, L6) in a batch-1 forward, throughput against context length shows the crossover clearly (Result 70). At short context attention is faster: at ctx 1024, attention runs 439K tok/s versus the brain's 245K tok/s. The ordering inverts as context grows, because the brain's throughput stays roughly constant while attention collapses: 1.4x at ctx 4096, 3.4x at ctx 16384, 11x at ctx 65536, and 21x at ctx 131072 (attention 30K tok/s, brain 633K tok/s) (Result 70). The 21x figure is therefore a long-context number, and the crossover means attention remains the right choice at short context.

### O(1) streaming memory

The constant-memory property holds in streaming and generation, not in the parallel forward. Feeding a fact at the start of a stream and recalling it at the end, carrying only the recurrent state between chunks, peak memory stays flat at 73 MB from 500 to 100K tokens (Result 32), because there is no growing KV cache. This is a genuine O(1) property, but it is bounded in recall horizon, not unbounded: recall is 100% at both 2K and 8K tokens, falls to 2% at 32K, and reaches 0% at 100K (Result 36). The recall horizon tracks the trained stream length rather than extrapolating past it. In the parallel (non-streaming) forward, activation memory scales with T for both the brain and attention, so there is no memory advantage there (Result 70); the O(1) win is specifically the streaming state versus a growing cache.

### Training efficiency

The training-step result (forward plus backward), measured brain versus attention (SDPA) with both models gradient-checkpointed, is a time and power win at parity memory (Result 72). With gradient checkpointing applied to both, memory is at parity (brain at 0.9x, effectively equal), while wall-clock time favors the brain: 6.5x faster at 32K, 11.7x at 65K, and 22.7x at 131K tokens (Result 72). To be exact about the honest reading: gradient checkpointing (torch checkpoint per block) is what removes the Taylor-feature (Fd=73) activation blowup and brings the brain's memory down to match attention, so the memory claim is parity and not a win. The linear-versus-quadratic time advantage is fundamental and checkpointing does not touch it. At short context (2K) the two are comparable (Result 72). The consequence for a fixed GPU budget is roughly 20x more long-context training on the brain than on a transformer, orthogonal to the separate 31% saving from growth (Result 72).

### Feasibility of a 1B model

The training-efficiency argument was carried through to a concrete 1.02B model (d=2048, L23) on an H100 NVL (96 GB, about 44 GB free with production co-resident) (Result 73). With gradient checkpointing the footprint is 19.1 GB and flat across batch size, which is what makes a 1B model fit alongside production at all; it would not fit without checkpointing (Result 73). Throughput moves from about 5K tok/s in fp32 (about 46 days) to 22.5K tok/s at batch 2 and 25.4K tok/s at batch 4 under bf16 autocast, at the same 19 GB, a 4.5x gain from bf16 (Result 73). This puts a Chinchilla-optimal 1B (20B tokens) at about 10 days from scratch and about 7 days with grow-to-1B, applying the 31% growth saving, with production staying up (Result 73). The bottleneck is throughput, not memory, at roughly 13% MFU, attributable to the shared GPU, the pure-PyTorch core, and checkpoint recompute; a dedicated GPU or a fused kernel would cut it further (Result 73). Two honest caveats: 20B tokens yields a compute-optimal but not overtrained 1B, and a strong 1B would need on the order of 1T tokens, which is months; and the bf16 path needs a short stability check before a full commitment, since the model was validated in fp32 (Result 73).

## SELF-GROWTH: cheaper by construction

The model does not have to be trained at full size from the start. It can begin small and cheap, then add capacity through a growth operator that is function-preserving: a newly added block computes an exact identity at the moment of insertion, so the network's outputs (and therefore its faculties) are unchanged the instant it grows, and training then continues from that point. This is the developmental premise of the project, and it survives measurement only in a specific, narrow form. We report both the negative and the positive plainly, because the difference between them is the whole result.

### Growth is not free capability

The first and most tempting version of the claim is false. An aggressive autonomous run grew the model 13 times over, from 18 layers to 31 layers (216M to 355M parameters), self-triggered and production-safe throughout, and capability stayed flat across the entire climb: held-out extractive recall bounced between 23 and 33 percent with no trend, and a full evaluation at the grown 344M size sat within noise of the ungrown 216M model on every axis (extractive question answering, needle retrieval, arithmetic, and grade-school math) (Result 48). Adding depth added parameters without adding capability. A bigger untrained network is still an untrained network, and the layers that were inserted as identities need training to become useful, which a fast growth cadence never gave them (Result 48).

### Why depth-growth cannot lift recall: capacity is state-bound

The deeper reason is architectural, and it is measured directly. Associative-recall capacity in this core is set by the size of the fast-weight state (the Taylor-feature dimension), not by depth. Holding depth fixed and widening the state carried recall from 9.3 percent to 100 percent, while pinning the state at a bottleneck and quadrupling depth (2 layers to 8) moved recall only from 9.1 percent to 9.9 percent (Result 49). A dedicated state-axis growth operator confirmed the lever: a function-preserving widening of the feature dimension, adversarially verified to be bit-exact at insertion (maximum logit change 4.8e-6), rescued recurrent-state recall from 25.6 percent to 99.7 percent, whereas adding four heavier layers reached only 32.8 percent despite adding 23 times more parameters (Result 50). Depth-growth therefore cannot improve recall by construction, which is the mechanistic root cause of the flat capability in Result 48.

### The positive result: growing is cheaper to a target

The claim that does survive is about compute, not capability. Measured as loss versus cumulative compute (parameters times tokens, the appropriate metric rather than loss versus step count), growing a model to a target loss costs less total compute than training the target size from scratch, because the early, cheap learning happens at small size before the model grows into its full width. Growing with the function-preserving identity operator reached the target for 17 percent less compute than from-scratch; copying trained layers into the new blocks (stacking) reached it for 22 percent less and also reached a marginally better final loss (Result 62). This is the first positive evidence for the actual thesis: the earlier scale negatives tested growth as free capability (correctly negative), whereas this tests growth as efficiency (Result 61).

Schedule turned out to be the dominant lever. Front-loading the learning while the model is small and cheap, then growing later (an early-heavy schedule), reached the target for 36 percent less compute; an equal-thirds schedule gave 28 percent and a late-heavy schedule only 17 percent (Result 65). Once the schedule is fixed to early-heavy, the operators converge on compute-to-target (identity, stacking, and hybrid all land near 34 percent cheaper), so the operator choice governs function-preservation and final quality rather than raw compute (Result 66).

### A hybrid operator that keeps both properties

Stacking is warm but not function-preserving: it copies trained layers, so outputs jump at the moment of growth (grow-jump loss change 0.142) and recover afterward. Identity is function-preserving (grow-jump 0) but starts each new block cold. A hybrid operator captures both: the new block is a copy of a trained block scaled by a learnable gain initialized to zero (a ReZero or LayerScale style construction applied to growth). It is function-preserving at insertion (grow-jump 0, faculties unchanged) and warm-started, reaching a final loss between stacking and identity (Result 66). The recipe, then, is an early-heavy schedule combined with the hybrid operator: cheaper to a target, function-preserving at every growth step, faculties intact throughout.

### It holds at larger scale

The efficiency saving is not a small-budget artifact. Re-run at roughly triple the compute and a larger model (width 512, 8 layers, 9000 steps), the early-heavy-plus-hybrid recipe reached the target for 31 percent less compute than from-scratch, close to the 34 to 36 percent seen in the smaller study, and the recall faculty stayed intact at 99 percent through growth at that width (Result 69). The absolute percentages remain indicative at these budgets and the relative orderings are the robust claim, but the direction is consistent across a threefold change in scale.

### Honest scope

Two limits stand. The saving is compute-to-target efficiency, not new capability: a grown model is cheaper to reach a given loss, but it is not more capable than a from-scratch model trained to the same loss (Result 48, Result 62). And the growth studied here is on the depth axis; the state-axis operator that actually governs recall capacity is proven function-preserving in isolation (Result 50) but a function-preserving width-grow in the live core remains an open axis. Within that scope, the developmental premise is validated: start small, grow cheaply, and keep the faculties intact by construction.

## Constant compute at any parameter count (sparse growth)

**Dense growth cannot hold compute constant.** Growing a model densely (adding width or depth that every token must traverse) raises the active FLOPs per token in proportion to the parameters added. Under a fixed compute budget this is the wall: a larger dense model costs strictly more per token to train and to run, so the goal of training as large a model as possible without spending more compute is unreachable by dense growth.

**Sparse growth keeps active compute flat while total parameters grow.** A sparse, top-1 expert (mixture-of-experts) growth operator breaks that coupling. Each token is routed to a single expert, so the active parameter count per token is fixed while the total parameter count rises. We measured this directly: active parameters per token stayed flat at 8.3M as the total grew from 8.3M to 19.4M across E=1 to E=8 experts (Result 74). Compute per token is therefore constant at any total parameter count, which is the property dense growth cannot provide.

**This is brain-like sparse activation.** Only a small fraction of a large parameter store is active for any given input, mirroring the sparse activation of biological cortex, and it is the sparse-growth analogue of the developmental depth and width growth studied elsewhere in this program.

**The recall faculty stays intact under MoE.** Adding experts does not damage the core memory faculty. On a clean reference harness, associative recall (MQAR with 16 pairs) is 100% for the plain core and remains 100% at both E=4 and E=8 (Result 74). Recall lives in the untouched VChunkRecall mixing path, so the mixture-of-experts layer composes with it at no cost. (An earlier 11% reading was traced to a misconfigured test harness, not to the MoE, and was corrected.)

### The honest limits

1. **The capability benefit is scale-gated.** Constant compute is proven, but sparse growth is not free capability. With Switch-style load balancing added, validation loss stayed flat to slightly worse as experts were added (4.88, 4.93, 4.97, 4.96 for E=1, 2, 4, 8; Result 74). At 8.3M parameters over 1800 steps the MoE capability benefit is absent; it is scale-gated, needing the billions of parameters and large data where mixture models are known to pay off. This is consistent with the program's standing finding that growth buys efficiency, not free capability.

2. **The wall-clock speedup needs a fused kernel.** The FLOP saving is real but does not show up in wall-clock time under a naive implementation. A MoE-1B (0.99B total, 166M active per token, E=8 top-1) trained at 22.7K tokens/s, the same as the dense-1B's 22.5K tokens/s, so the same roughly 10-day budget rather than faster (Result 75). The active FLOPs are about 6x lower (166M versus 1B active), but the naive masked-loop dispatch fragments the matmul into many small irregular expert GEMMs plus gather and scatter, collapsing wall-clock back to dense speed. A capacity-based dispatch that batches each expert's tokens into a single matmul recovers part of this gap, but realizing the full roughly 6x (on the order of 2 to 3 days for 20B tokens) requires a fused grouped-GEMM / expert-parallel kernel. Because torch.compile corrupts this core, that is real Triton or CUDA engineering, not a configuration change: the blocker is the kernel, not the concept (Result 75).

3. **There is a storage ceiling that sparse activation does not solve.** Constant compute is not constant storage: every expert's parameters must still be held in memory even though only one is active per token. Total parameters therefore ceiling at what fits in memory (on the order of 100B). Sparse activation addresses compute, not this storage wall. The path beyond it is external retrieval memory, that is, the recall faculty (knowledge held in the fast-weight state or store rather than in weights), which gives bounded weights and compute with unbounded knowledge (Result 74). MoE for compute and external memory for storage compose cleanly.

## HONEST LIMITS AND NEGATIVE RESULTS

The consistent finding across this work is that every efficiency and growth mechanism is demonstrable and provable at small scale, but the capability payoff of each is gated by scale (and, for sparse constant-compute growth, by a missing fused kernel). We report the negatives plainly.

**Language transfer required both scale and the right objective.** In-context recall, the core intrinsic faculty, is proven on synthetic tokens but did not transfer to natural prose below roughly 45M parameters. The honest reading of that early result was a scale threshold, not a mechanism failure (Result 38). At 216M and about 2.3B tokens the faculty evaluation showed genuine short-range signal (extractive SQuAD-style QA 30%, HellaSwag 35%, SciQ 42.5%), but two critical negatives stood out: long-context needle recall collapsed with distance (8% at 256 tokens, 8% at 512, 0% at 1024, 0% at 1792), and long-form greedy generation degenerated to 92% 4-gram repetition (Result 45).

**More tokens did not fix long context; the wall was language, not the mechanism.** A clean 18-layer run trained to roughly 4B tokens (Chinchilla-optimal) improved fluency (4-gram repetition fell from 92% to 60%) but left the needle curve flat and noisy at 24/12/4/0 across 256/512/1024/1792 tokens, even with long-needle training data added (Result 52). A diagnostic then isolated the cause: on synthetic single-needle and dense-distractor tasks the recall core scores 100% (across 1024 tokens of intervening noise and 128 competing bindings), while the identical query on real prose at 1792 tokens scored 0%. The bottleneck was language comprehension at 216M, not the recall substrate, which retired the forget-gate, added-state-capacity, and growth hypotheses for long context (Result 53).

**Scaling attempts came back negative or scale-gated.** A manual, function-preserving depth-heavy scale-up to 400M failed to beat the 216M after about 2.7B tokens: its predictive-entropy signal never crossed the 216M's converged floor, and downstream scores were worse or equal (extractive QA 20% versus 27%). Doubling depth made the model harder to train rather than more capable, confirming that depth was the wrong growth axis (Result 56).

**The objective, not added capacity, surfaced the latent skill, but this does not yield a product.** Answer-masked retrieval SFT on the 216M base moved needle@1792 from 0% to 12% in about 15 minutes with zero new parameters and no scaling (Result 57). A balanced variant with 50% replay took the needle curve to 40/24/32/24 across 256/512/1024/1792 tokens while preserving knowledge (SciQ 42% to 50%, history 37% to 45%) (Result 58). This proved the long-context gap was a skill that pretraining had not surfaced rather than a capacity the model lacked. It did not make the 216M a usable general or coding product: a usability test scored 55% (5 of 9) on reading comprehension with fact-confusion, corrupted needles past roughly 430 tokens, and unreliable instruction following (Result 67), and on a realistic multi-fact knowledge-base document the model answered only 3 of 12 clear-fact questions (25%), consistently selecting the wrong fact (Result 71). The single-fact successes were misleading; realistic document-QA is unreliable at this size.

## DISCUSSION AND CONCLUSION

Taken together, the results describe one coherent pattern rather than a collection of setbacks. The intrinsic recall faculty transfers to natural prose once the training objective pressures it and once the model is large enough to comprehend the prose (the first short-range signal appears at 216M once a recall-mix pressures retrieval, Result 44, later mapped in Result 45). Long-context recall is present latently at 216M and is surfaced by balanced retrieval-SFT without any scaling (Results 57 and 58). Depth-heavy growth alone does not add capability (Result 56), and neither more tokens nor added state capacity nor a forget-gate lifts long-context recall when the true limit is language comprehension (Results 52 and 53). In every case the mechanism works and is provable small, while the capability payoff is gated by scale (or, for the sparse constant-compute path, by a fused kernel that pure-PyTorch dispatch cannot substitute for).

The deliverable, therefore, is a validated research method and recipe, not a product. The method is: an efficient recurrent recall core, function-preserving growth applied on the axis that actually carries the target capability rather than depth for its own sake, and balanced retrieval-SFT (answer-masked tasks with roughly 50% replay) to surface latent long-context recall without eroding existing knowledge (Results 57 and 58). We do not claim a general assistant or a coding model: at 216M the artifact is a research prototype suitable for capability demonstrations, and document-QA at that size is unreliable (Results 67 and 71).

What remains is threefold. First, scale: a larger and better-trained core is the identified lever for the language comprehension and long-range retrieval that the 216M cannot reach (Results 45, 52, 53, and 56). Second, a fused kernel for sparse constant-compute growth, so that the theoretical compute saving is realized in wall-clock rather than absorbed by dispatch overhead. Third, external retrieval memory, so that knowledge can grow beyond what a fixed weight budget can store while the weights and compute stay bounded. The through-line stands: the mechanisms are proven, the recipe is validated, and the open question is no longer whether the approach works in principle but whether it pays off at a scale we have not yet trained.
